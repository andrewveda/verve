A short study in questions

Yes/No Questions

The simplest question of all — and one of the most useful.

Do you want to take the first test?

Yes, test 1 No, I will take test 2 now!

A Yes/No question is a type of question that can only be answered with a yes or a no. Nothing more is required, and nothing more is expected — the question is built to accept exactly one of two possible replies.

Also called

A closed question, because it closes the door on any answer besides "yes" or "no." It limits the possible responses to precisely these two options, unlike an open question, which invites a free, unlimited answer.

You'll recognise a Yes/No question by its shape, not just its meaning. In English, most of them are formed by moving a helping verb — do, is, can, have, and so on — to the front of the sentence, ahead of the subject. That inversion is what signals to the listener: a short answer is all I'm asking for.

A taxonomy

Nine forms a closed question can take

I

Basic auxiliary questions

Built with do, does, or did, placed before the subject. This is the everyday, all-purpose Yes/No question for ordinary verbs.

Do you drink coffee?
Yes, every morning. / No, never.
II

Be-verb questions

Formed by moving is, are, was, or were to the front. No extra helping verb is needed, since be does the inverting itself.

Is the store still open?
Yes, until nine. / No, it closed early.
III

Modal questions

Use can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, or must. These ask about ability, permission, possibility, or obligation — but the answer is still a plain yes or no.

Could you send that file today?
Sure, yes. / No, not today.
IV

Have/perfect questions

Formed with have or has, asking whether something is already complete.

Have you finished the report?
Yes, it's done. / No, not yet.
V

Negative questions

Phrased in the negative — isn't, don't, haven't — usually to express surprise, or to check an assumption the speaker already leans toward.

Isn't it a little late to call?
Yes, it is. / No, it's fine.
VI

Tag questions

A statement with a small Yes/No question tacked onto the end, used to confirm something the speaker already believes.

You're coming to the party, aren't you?
Yes, I'll be there. / No, I can't make it.
VII

Embedded (indirect) questions

A Yes/No question folded inside a larger, more polite sentence, introduced by if or whether. Word order stays like a normal statement.

Could you tell me if the train has left?
Yes, it has. / No, not yet.
VIII

"Or not" questions

Adds or not to the end. It looks like it offers a choice, but it's still fully closed — the two options were yes and no all along.

Are you joining us, or not?
Yes, I am. / No, I'm not.
IX

Closed items in forms and surveys

Outside of conversation, Yes/No questions show up as checkboxes and radio buttons — on intake forms, ballots, and research surveys — wherever a clean, countable answer matters more than a nuanced one.

Do you consent to receive updates by email?

Know the difference

Closed vs. open questions

Closed question

Limits the reply to a fixed, small set of answers — usually just yes or no.

"Did you enjoy the film?"

Open question

Invites a free, unrestricted answer, often starting with what, why, or how.

"What did you think of the film?"
Where they're used

You'll meet them everywhere

  • Everyday small talk and quick check-ins
  • Job interviews screening for a fact
  • Medical intake and consent forms
  • Customer surveys and feedback polls
  • Referendums and yes/no ballots
  • Classroom checks for understanding
  • Legal depositions and sworn statements
  • App onboarding and permission prompts
